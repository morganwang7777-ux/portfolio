ESP32 wireless telemetry

Prototype for the TCU redesign · Oct 2026

  • UW Formula Electric
  • ESP32
  • C++
  • Python
  • SQL
  • SQLite

View on GitHub

  • 18range test runs, about 8,300 packets
  • ≤ 0.4%packet loss at most positions up to 15 m
  • 42.7%of all packets lost at 10 m behind a closed door
  • 5.8 slongest dropout

What

The previous UW Formula Electric TCU sent vehicle data over a cellular modem and SIM card, which added a monthly cost and was unreliable at racetracks. Before comparing non-cellular options on paper, I built a small Wi-Fi prototype to get real data on how far a low-cost link can go before data is lost, and whether it fails gradually or in bursts.

How

An ESP32 reads a potentiometer standing in for a throttle sensor, shows live status on an I2C OLED, and sends about 17.5 UDP packets per second. A Python logger on the laptop stores every packet in SQLite. Each packet carries a sequence number, so lost packets show up as gaps. I tested 6 conditions, 3 runs of 30 s each, and used SQL queries and matplotlib to analyze them.

Result

Loss stayed at 0.4% or less at most positions up to 15 m, but reached 12% at one 10 m spot and 42.7% behind a closed door. Losses came in bursts of up to 5.8 s, and loss rose sharply once the signal fell below about -80 dBm.

What it means for the TCU

Basic Wi-Fi with an on-board antenna is far short of racetrack range. The new TCU needs a radio built for range, such as LoRa or a 900 MHz link, an external antenna mounted high on the car, and a buffer that stores data during dropouts and resends it.

How it works

[Potentiometer] --ADC--> [ESP32] --Wi-Fi (UDP)--> [Laptop: logger.py] --> [SQLite] --> [plot.py]
[OLED display]  <--I2C--    |
                            └-- USB serial: wired backup and debugging

Each packet is a small JSON message, for example {"seq":120,"t_ms":8034,"throttle":25,"rssi":-54}. The phone hotspot was only used as a local access point, so the ESP32 and laptop talked directly without going through the internet or the cellular network.

Analysis in SQL

Every run is stored in an SQLite database. Packet loss comes from the sequence numbers: the expected count is the last minus the first sequence number plus one, and anything missing was lost. Excerpt from summary.sql:

SELECT r.id AS run, r.note,
       COUNT(s.seq)                AS samples,
       MAX(s.seq) - MIN(s.seq) + 1 AS expected,
       ROUND(AVG(s.rssi), 1)       AS avg_rssi_dbm
FROM runs r
JOIN samples s ON s.run_id = r.id
GROUP BY r.id;

What I found

  • At most positions up to 15 m, the link was solid. The exception was one 10 m spot that averaged 12% loss.
  • Losses come in bursts. In the bad runs, some seconds received all 17 packets and others received none.
  • The two worst runs averaged below -80 dBm and lost 37% and 77% of packets.
  • Placement mattered more than distance. My 10 m spot did worse than 15 m, and the 5 m door spot had a stronger signal than the open 5 m spot.

Limitations

  • Loss is counted between the first and last packet received, so a dropout at the very end of a run is not counted.
  • This was a single, controlled test that didn't account for other variables, and distances were estimated based on footstep size.
  • RSSI is the ESP32's view of the phone's signal, not the other way round.

Next steps

  • Repeat the range test with a LoRa module to compare against Wi-Fi.
  • Buffer packets on the ESP32 and resend them after a dropout.
  • Add more sensors and move the circuit onto a PCB.

IMD test box

  • 147 kΩcritical insulation threshold

What

Developing a passive insulation monitoring fault injection test box for the Formula Electric vehicle, interfacing through the HV+/HV- Tractive System Measuring Points (TSMP) and chassis reference for controlled test conditions.

How

Created Altium Designer schematics and resistor networks, accounting for the 15 kOhm TSMP protection resistance. No single resistor met both the power and voltage ratings at 588 V, so I split each fault path into a 10-resistor series chain, keeping every 2512 resistor under 0.4 W and 60 V. The test positions, target fault resistances and expected IMD responses come from a chart provided by the electrical team leads, and I designed the box to meet them.

Result

Designed resistor networks around the leads' 147 kOhm critical insulation threshold, for target equivalent fault resistances of 285, 485, and 75 kOhm. Planned selection states include PASS, WARNING, TECH FAIL, and a hard short scenario. The design provides a repeatable method to investigate firmware reported warnings and vehicle shutdown responses.

Next steps

Currently under review by the electrical team leads. Physical testing on the car will follow.

5 V DC-DC buck converter

Design task · Sep – Oct 2026

  • LTspice
  • Altium Designer
  • DigiKey
  • PCB Design

View on GitHub

  • 23.6 mVppoutput ripple (v2)
  • 48.3%less ripple than v1
  • 41.4 × 23.6 mmtwo layer SMD board

What

Designed a compact 10-15 V input to regulated 5 V, 1 A switching buck converter using the LT1375 for a low voltage power supply design task.

How

Selected a 15 uH inductor, Schottky freewheeling diode, input/output capacitors, and resistive feedback components using datasheets and calculations. Simulated switching behavior and component trade offs in LTspice.

Result

Reduced simulated output ripple from 45.7 mVpp (v1) to 23.6 mVpp (48.3%, v2) by changing output capacitor ESR from 0.10 to 0.05 Ohm and tuning compensation (1 kOhm / 3.3 nF) at 15 V input and 1 A load.

PCB

Designed a 41.4 x 23.6 mm two layer SMD layout in Altium Designer with 25 mil power traces, 10 mil signal traces, a bottom layer GND polygon, and via connected returns.

From v1 to v2

V1 used a 15 uH inductor, a 100 uF output capacitor with 0.10 ohm modeled ESR, and a 3.3 nF VC compensation capacitor.

For V2, I decreased R4 from 0.1 Ω to 0.05 Ω to reduce the output capacitor's ESR. Since the capacitor handles changing current when the buck converter switches, having less resistance means less voltage fluctuation and therefore lower ripple. I also added a 1 kΩ resistor to the compensation circuit to help control how the IC responds to voltage changes and prevent oscillations.

The independent effect of each modification has not yet been fully isolated.

PCB layout and design rule check

Component placement: Grouped the LT1375, input capacitor, Schottky diode, BOOST circuitry, and inductor to reduce switching-path lengths.

Power routing: The DigiKey trace width calculator gives a 20.7 mil minimum for 1.5 A on an external 1 oz layer (10 °C rise, 40 °C ambient), so major power connections use 25 mil traces. Feedback and compensation signals use about 10 mil.

Ground plane: Created a Bottom Layer GND polygon pour and connected ground pads using short traces and vias.

DRC: 0 violations across 14 Altium rules, including short-circuit, un-routed nets, 10–30 mil trace width, 10 mil hole-to-hole clearance and 6 mil solder mask sliver.

Morgan's Metronome v1.0

What

Built an Arduino UNO based digital metronome after finding myself getting distracted using my phone while practicing saxophone. Designed it to help maintain a consistent rhythm, featuring adjustable 40–180 BPM tempo, selectable beat patterns, pushbutton controls, a potentiometer, and a 16×2 LCD.

How

Programmed the timing and user interface in C++. Used millis() based timing to schedule beats so the tempo does not drift; the loop only pauses for the 60 ms beep, so the tempo and button controls stay responsive. Prototyped and debugged the circuit on a breadboard before designing a custom Arduino UNO shield PCB in KiCad to integrate the components.

Result

Developed a functional metronome with an audible buzzer and visual LED beat indicators, adjustable tempo, and selectable rhythm patterns. Will order and test the PCB soon.

Try it in the browser

Same logic as the Arduino code: 40–180 BPM, accented first beat, 2–7 beat patterns.

Full design detailsDevelopment, firmware, pin map, shield PCB and enclosure

Development

The project started as a simple buzzer and LED metronome.

I originally used delay() for timing, but testing against a reference metronome showed that the BPM was inaccurate. I replaced this with millis()-based timing so the beat interval could be tracked more reliably.

I also replaced button-based BPM control with a potentiometer. Since the analog input was too sensitive, I rounded the BPM to 5 BPM increments.

After finishing the breadboard prototype I recreated the circuit in KiCad, ran ERC/DRC checks, selected and measured component footprints, routed the PCB, added a bottom GND plane and generated Gerber files.

Firmware

The beat is scheduled with millis(), so the tempo does not drift. The loop only pauses for the 60 ms beep (delay(60) below), so the buttons and potentiometer stay responsive. Excerpt from MorgansMetronome.ino:

void loop() {
  int potValue = analogRead(A5);
  int newBpm = map(potValue, 0, 1023, 40, 180);
  newBpm = ((newBpm + 2) / 5) * 5;   // round to 5 BPM steps
  ...
  if (running) {
    unsigned long currentTime = millis();
    if (currentTime - lastBeat >= beatInterval) {
      lastBeat += beatInterval;
      currentBeat++;
      if (currentBeat >= patternLength) currentBeat = 0;
      digitalWrite(8, HIGH);
      if (currentBeat == 0) tone(9, 400);   // accented first beat
      else tone(9, 200);
      delay(60);
      digitalWrite(8, LOW);
      noTone(9);
    }
  }
}
